\chapter*{Conclusion}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Conclusion}

The aim of this thesis was to understand, study, analyze, and implement
classical knapsack cryptosystems. The thesis did not focus only on the original
Merkle--Hellman variant, but also analyzed and compared selected related
variants by looking at their design, efficiency, and known weaknesses. The main
motivation was to show why the difficulty of the underlying mathematical
problem is not enough to assume that a cryptosystem is secure, especially when
the trapdoor is based on an easy structured version of that problem.

The theoretical part first introduced cryptographic and complexity-theory
background needed for the topic. Special focus was given to the distinction
between general subset-sum hardness and the structured instances used in
cryptosystems. It then described subset-sum as the underlying mathematical
problem for the Merkle--Hellman cryptosystem. After defining the problem, it
explained that the decision version of subset-sum is NP-complete and why this
fact was important for early knapsack cryptography. The role of the
superincreasing private sequence and modular multiplication was explained
before moving to the individual phases of the system: key generation,
encryption, and decryption. The later sections discussed why the basic
construction does not hide its trapdoor structure well enough and is therefore
vulnerable. The main attacks described were Shamir's structural attack and
low-density lattice-based attacks. The thesis also described selected variants,
including permuted and iterated Merkle--Hellman constructions and the
Chor--Rivest knapsack-type cryptosystem, together with their known
cryptanalysis.

The practical part of the thesis focused on implementing the classical
Merkle--Hellman cryptosystem and selected variants in a modular C program. The
implementation was not intended to be a secure cryptographic library, but
rather an experimental tool with separate modes for demonstration,
benchmarking, and simple solver experiments. The program implements complete
process of key generation, encryption and decryption. The benchmark mode
measured running times of these stages and recorded values such as density,
modulus margin, and the bit length of the private-weight sum. The solver
experiments tested direct subset-sum methods, such as brute force and
meet-in-the-middle search, on small generated instances. 

The implementation also required choices that are not fixed by the mathematical
description of the Merkle--Hellman scheme. These choices included how the
superincreasing private sequence is generated, how much margin is added before
choosing the modulus, and how the multiplier is sampled and checked for
invertibility modulo \(m\). These details were useful for the experiments,
because they directly influence the generated weights, density, and
key-generation behaviour.

The experiments verified the implementation and connected it with the
theoretical discussion. The benchmarks showed that key generation was the main
cost, the permuted variant behaved close to the classical variant, and extra
iterated layers increased runtime while lowering density. The parameter sweeps
also showed that the private-sequence generator influences the generated
instances, especially for smaller block sizes.

The simple brute-force and meet-in-the-middle solvers illustrated the
exponential growth of direct subset-sum solving. However, they are only
educational baselines. The real weakness of Merkle--Hellman comes from the
structure of the trapdoor and from attacks that exploit this structure.

The main conclusion is that classical knapsack cryptosystems are not secure
public-key encryption systems, even though they are related to an NP-complete
problem. Their value today is mainly historical and educational: they show that
a hard underlying problem is not enough if the generated public instances still
contain exploitable structure.

The contribution of this thesis is therefore not a new cryptosystem, but a
structured study of classical knapsack cryptography. It gives a chronological
overview of the main constructions and attacks, summarizes the main reason why
these systems failed, and connects the theory with a working implementation and
experiments. Subset-sum itself remains an active theoretical and algorithmic
problem, but classical trapdoor knapsack cryptosystems are no longer considered
practical public-key encryption schemes. This contrast is one of the main
lessons of the topic.
