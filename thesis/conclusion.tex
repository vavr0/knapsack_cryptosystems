\chapter*{Conclusion}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Conclusion}

The aim of this thesis was to study and implement classical knapsack
cryptosystems. The thesis did not focus only on the original Merkle--Hellman
variant, but also analyzed and compared selected related variants by looking at
their design, efficiency, known weaknesses, and attacks. The motivation was to
understand why basing a cryptosystem on an NP-complete problem is not enough
for security when the system generates structured public instances. The
implementation and experiments were used to compare the selected variants and
to observe how parameter choices affect the generated knapsack instances.

The theoretical part first introduced cryptographic and complexity-theory
background needed for the topic. Special focus was given to the distinction
between general subset-sum hardness and the structured instances used in
cryptosystems. It then described subset-sum as the underlying mathematical
problem for the Merkle--Hellman cryptosystem. After defining the problem, it
explained that the decision version of subset-sum is NP-complete and why this
fact was important for early knapsack cryptography. The role of the
superincreasing private sequence and modular multiplication was explained
before moving to the individual phases of the system. The later sections
discussed why the basic construction does not hide its trapdoor structure well
enough and is therefore vulnerable. Shamir's attack was presented as a
structural attack that can recover an equivalent trapdoor, while low-density
lattice attacks were presented as direct attacks on suitable subset-sum
instances. The thesis also described selected variants, including permuted and
iterated Merkle--Hellman constructions and the Chor--Rivest knapsack-type
cryptosystem, together with their known cryptanalysis.

The practical part of the thesis focused on implementing the classical
Merkle--Hellman cryptosystem and selected variants in the C programming
language. The implementation was not intended to be a secure cryptographic
library, but rather an experimental tool with separate modes for demonstration,
benchmarking, and solver experiments. The program implements the complete
process of key generation, encryption, and decryption. The benchmark mode
measured running times of these stages and recorded values such as density,
modulus margin, and the bit length of the private-weight sum. The solver
experiments tested direct subset-sum methods, such as brute force and
meet-in-the-middle search, on small generated instances. 

The implementation also required choices that are not fixed by the mathematical
description of the Merkle--Hellman scheme. These choices included how the
superincreasing private sequence is generated, how much margin is added before
choosing the modulus, and how the multiplier is sampled and checked for
invertibility modulo \(m\). These details were useful for the experiments,
because they directly influence the generated weights, density, and
key-generation behaviour.

The experiments showed how the implemented constructions behave for the tested
parameters. The benchmarks showed that key generation was the main cost. The permuted
variant behaved close to the classical variant, and extra iterated layers
increased runtime while lowering density. The parameter sweeps also showed that
the private-sequence generator influences the generated instances, especially
for smaller block sizes, while the modulus margin had little visible effect on
the reported density.

The brute-force and meet-in-the-middle solvers illustrated the
exponential growth of direct subset-sum solving. However, they are only
educational baselines. The real weakness of Merkle--Hellman comes from the
structure of the trapdoor and from attacks that exploit this structure.

The main conclusion is that classical knapsack cryptosystems are not secure
public-key encryption systems. Their public keys are generated from a
superincreasing private structure, and this structure can leak information that
helps an attacker recover an equivalent trapdoor or solve ciphertexts directly.
This shows why security cannot be concluded only from the hardness of the
general subset-sum problem.

The contribution of this thesis is not a new cryptosystem, but a structured
study of classical knapsack cryptography. It gives a chronological overview of
the main constructions and attacks, summarizes the main reason why these
systems failed, and connects the theory with a working implementation and
experiments. Subset-sum itself remains an active theoretical and algorithmic
problem, but classical trapdoor knapsack cryptosystems are no longer considered
practical public-key encryption schemes. This makes them useful mainly as an
example of how a cryptographic construction can fail even when it is based on a
difficult general problem.
